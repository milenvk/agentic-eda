== Workflow Catalog

Six business workflows (W1 to W6), two supporting workflows (S1, S2), and the cross-cutting
layers of chapters 10 to 15. Each workflow is an event-flow narrative; "built in" lists the
chapters that construct it. A workflow built across several chapters is the norm: every
component is complete from its first chapter, and each later chapter's compose file switches on
the step it teaches. Event names are defined in the catalog at the end of this document; the
nouns are the glossary's in 01_system_overview.adoc.

=== W1: Trip Booking (the spine)

The one workflow that runs through the whole book. Every part extends it rather than replacing
it. Intake is its first step, not a workflow of its own: until the request is written the
conversation belongs to Customer Relations. The correlation id is the conversation's id, generated
at the first customer message and inherited by everything that follows; every booking,
planning, and policy fact after `booking.TripRequested` carries the trip's id as its partition
key, and `TripRequested` and every customer fact the conversation's. The chapter in brackets is where a step first runs. Before ch 3 the demo
script stands in for the Booking Agent; in ch 3 it stands in for the customer, writing booking's
requests and printing what booking publishes; the Front Desk takes that seat in ch 4.

*End-state flow:*

1. **The customer asks** [4]. The customer writes in the chat. The Front Desk records
   `customer.ChatMessageReceived` (the conversation id, the text, the channel); its intent
   classifier recognises a trip request and writes `booking.TripRequested` in booking's
   published language: the origin-destinations, each with its date or a window (one for a
   one-way trip, two for a round trip, several for a multi-city one), the stays, the party,
   a budget, and the preferences in the customer's own words. Its causation id is the chat message and its
   partition key the conversation's id, since no trip exists yet. When `booking.TripCreated`
   arrives on the conversation's correlation id, the Front Desk records the link from the
   conversation to the trip and follows the trip's facts from here on. Nobody outside Customer Relations reads raw chat. In ch 3 the demo script writes
   `TripRequested`; the Router writes it for email from ch 4.
2. **Booking validates and asks planning** [3]. The Booking Agent consumes `TripRequested`,
   checks it as a business request (dates in the future and in order, at least one
   origin-destination, the party stated, a budget or an explicit "no limit"). A request that fails gets
   `booking.TripRequestRejected` (what is missing or wrong), which the Front Desk shows as a
   question; no itinerary is ever planned for it. A request that passes is recorded as the Trip
   under a store-assigned id, with the request's event id kept beside it under a unique index
   so a redelivered request finds its trip instead of creating a second one; announced by
   `booking.TripCreated` (the trip's id, the accepted request, causation pointing at
   `TripRequested`, and the trip's id as the key from here on); and sent to planning as
   `planning.ItineraryRequested`: the trip id as the key, the validated requirements, causation
   pointing at `TripRequested`. Booking asks planning in planning's own language, the open-host
   pattern one hop in, and keeps no counter: any caller may ask, and the planning topic's
   per-key lane orders a trip's requests. In ch 1 and 2 the demo script writes
   `ItineraryRequested` itself, with a trip id of its own generation.
3. **Planning proposes itineraries** [1]. The Planner consumes `ItineraryRequested`, its only
   inbound request, and does all of its work inside: it asks the Research Agent over A2A what
   destination knowledge holds about each stay's city [3]; the flight-search sub-agent
   searches each requested origin-destination, across the airports of a city code and with or
   without a stop; the hotel-search sub-agent matches the preferences per stay against the
   hotel sim's inventory over MCP and flags a last room; the validator rejects anything breaking a hard constraint,
   including a flight leaving before the one before it has landed; the
   planner node assembles and ranks. It publishes `planning.ItineraryProposed`: the trip id, the
   proposal's id (its request's), and one to three ranked itineraries, each with a label, its
   items in travel order with their offers and validity, a total, and a rationale. A multi-city
   itinerary is the same structure with more items. A trip accumulates proposals; none is
   "current", and the customer may choose from any of them.
4. **The Negotiator argues the rates** [7]. The Hotel Negotiator consumes `ItineraryProposed`
   and opens one negotiation per distinct stay across the proposal's itineraries, each in its own activation
   over A2A with that hotel's agent, several turns each. Each ends in `booking.RateNegotiated`
   (the stay, the hotel, the list rate, the negotiated rate, the terms, the validity). The
   Booking Agent's barrier is set to the number of distinct stays; in ch 7 the simulator's
   auto-responders answer at once, and from ch 8 the `EventScheduler` times out the stragglers.
5. **Booking declares the proposal ready** [3]. The Booking Agent records the proposal and
   publishes `booking.ProposalReady`: the trip id, the proposal's id, and the itineraries as the
   customer should see them, at once until ch 7 and, from ch 7, when the barrier
   releases, each stay at its negotiated rate. The Front Desk holds one subscription to the
   booking topic filtered by its trips; `ProposalReady` exists so it never joins two types.
6. **The customer sees the itineraries** [4]. The Front Desk shows the itineraries with their
   offers' validity and records `customer.CustomerNotified` (the conversation id, the trip id,
   what was shown, when). The Booking Agent never reads a `customer.*` type; from ch 8 it
   schedules `booking.CustomerFollowUpDue` from its own `ProposalReady` and nudges if no reply
   comes. In ch 1 and 2 the demo script receives `ItineraryProposed` and prints the itineraries;
   in ch 3 it receives `ProposalReady`.
7. **The customer chooses** [3]. The customer replies in the chat, another
   `ChatMessageReceived`; the Front Desk knows which trip the conversation concerns, and its
   classifier writes `booking.CustomerReplied` in booking's published language: the trip id,
   the proposal it answers, the recognised intent with its content (chose itinerary two; too
   expensive; make it the 14th to the 17th). The Booking Agent consumes it and, as the Trip's
   one writer, commits one of three facts: `booking.ItineraryApproved` (the proposal's id and
   the chosen itinerary's rank, from any of the trip's proposals),
   `booking.ItineraryRejected` (the customer's reason), or `booking.TripChanged` (the changed
   requirements). After a rejection or a change it asks planning again with a new
   `ItineraryRequested`: the requirements as they now stand, the customer's reason, and from
   ch 7 the items that carry over, named by proposal and rank (the partial re-plan). The Planner
   reads its own store for what it already proposed, has one inbound type, and never reacts to
   booking's facts except `ItineraryApproved`, which marks the chosen itinerary in its proposal.
8. **Policy** [8]. The Policy Gate consumes `ItineraryApproved`, reads the corporate client's
   seeded rules, and publishes `policy.PolicyCheckPassed` or `policy.PolicyViolationDetected`,
   which opens W4. The reservation step runs on the pass or on the manager's decision: two
   independent keys, neither of them the agent's own reasoning. Before ch 8 no Gate runs and the
   Booking Agent's configuration lets the step proceed on `ItineraryApproved` alone.
9. **Reservation step** [7]. The Booking Agent's deterministic reservation step reserves what
   the accepted itinerary contains, from the proposal it received, on the Airline and Hotel
   Reservation System sims, each hold
   committed as its own fact before the next: `booking.FlightReserved` and
   `booking.HotelReserved` (at the negotiated rate), holds with an expiry. A reservation no
   longer granted by the supplier is `booking.ReservationFailed`; the step falls back to the
   stay item's backup when it carries one, and otherwise the Booking Agent asks the customer
   through `ProposalReady`. The Rental Car Agent reacts to `FlightReserved` by pure
   choreography: a pay-later `booking.CarRentalReserved` in the requested class with its
   estimated total, or `booking.CarRentalUnavailable` when no class fits; the Booking Agent
   records the car on the trip with `booking.CarReserved`, causation set to the car event. The
   trip is now reserved.
10. **Payment** [10]. The Booking Agent publishes `booking.PaymentRequested` (the amount, an
    idempotency key); the payment provider adapter charges the Payment sim under that key and
    answers with `booking.PaymentAuthorized`; the Booking Agent records the Payment entity on
    the trip.
11. **Confirmation** [8]. `booking.TripConfirmed` (Booking Agent), carrying the customer
    reference: the holds are converted to confirmed reservations with the suppliers. The Front
    Desk shows it and records `customer.CustomerNotified`. In ch 8 and ch 9 confirmation means
    the reservations confirmed, with payment offstage; ch 10 inserts step 10 before it, and the
    refund step of W5 with it.

*Built in:* ch 1 (steps 2, 3, and 6 as a round trip: the demo script writes
`ItineraryRequested` and receives `ItineraryProposed`, matching on the request event's id; the
audit consumer; one topic per context and per-key lanes in the adapter), ch 2 (the LangGraph
Planner replacing the plain one on the same events; CloudEvents contracts, correlation and
causation, `partitionkey`), ch 3 (the Booking Agent takes planning's end and the demo script the
customer's: steps 2, 5, and 7; the Research Agent over A2A in step 3), ch 4 (the Front Desk and
the Router take the customer's seat: steps 1 and 6 for chat and email), ch 7 (steps 4 and 9 and
the partial re-plan: the demonstration ends with the trip reserved, both holds in place), ch 8
(steps 8 and 11, the follow-up nudge), ch 10 (step 10, idempotent).

==== The ch 7 demonstration: two Paris runs

Ch 7's practical runs W1 twice through steps 3, 4, 5, 7, and 9, with every agent up, no
seeding, and real events; the audit consumer's recording of the first run is ch 12's historical
event file and an optional replay shortcut for readers who skip the first run. The
demonstration uses only what the book has introduced by ch 7, and it does not spend ch 9's
lesson early: a lapsing hold is not a saga.

*First run.* A customer asks for two travelers, New York to Paris, 12 to 15 March, a hotel in
the 8th arrondissement. The Planner's hotel-search sub-agent finds one room in the 8th and flags
it as the last room from the sim's rooms-left field; the validator settles arrival against
check-in; the proposal carries three itineraries. The Negotiator argues each itinerary's stay
with the hotel's agent, the barrier releases, and the customer sees three prices already argued
down, with their validity. The customer chooses the 8th after twenty-five minutes. The
reservation step holds the flight, and the hotel reservation fails (`ReservationFailed`): the
last room is sold. `ProposalReady` tells the customer "sold out, your flight is held until
18:00, shall I look nearby?"; the customer answers "try the 16th", which the Front Desk writes
as `CustomerReplied` and the Booking Agent commits as `TripChanged`, asking planning again with
the flight item marked as carried over. The Planner re-plans the stay only; the
flight hold carries over; the customer chooses; the hotel is reserved; the run ends with the
trip reserved, both holds in place. The human fixed it.

*The lesson.* A practice, not a frequency claim, as a human agent would draw it from one case.
The experiential Observer writes under `/trips/*/paris/hotel`: a hotel offer flagged as the last
room was gone by the time the customer chose; when a hotel offer is flagged last room, carry a
backup hotel on the stay item and tell the customer the offer's validity. The lesson is filed
where it was learned, and the path scoping is the epistemic humility: one case earns a local
practice, not a global rule. The lesson's wording varies by run; ch 12's fixtures pin it in
tests.

*Second run.* Another customer asks for the 8th on similar dates. The Planner hydrates the
lesson at wake-up (ch 6's port, now filled) and its hotel-search sub-agent proposes the 8th
room, flagged last room, with a backup in the 16th on the stay item; the customer is slow again;
the reservation step tries the 8th, fails the same way, reserves the backup without asking, and
`ProposalReady` tells the customer; the run ends with the trip reserved. The world did not
change; the handling did.

*What the design supplies:* a rooms-left field on hotel inventory; a backup on the stay item;
the reservation step falling back to a backup on failure; `booking.ReservationFailed`; hold
expiry as a reservation status; a partial re-plan as a new request naming the items that carry
over.

==== The ch 7 interrupt: a newer request mid-planning

A relevant fact can arrive while a long activation runs, and the agent should revise its
beliefs rather than finish on stale ones. The book's vocabulary is *interrupting events*, with
three strategies named: defer to the next activation (safe, and stale), inject the fact as an
observation at the next reasoning step (belief revision inside the loop), or abort and restart
from the checkpoint plus the fact (the boundary event, wasteful of tokens). Other traditions
have their own names: belief revision with intention reconsideration (BDI), the interrupting
boundary event (BPMN), a signal to a running workflow (Temporal), an interrupt (agent
frameworks, usually meaning a pause for human input).

The mechanism is the activator's activation inbox (`04_implementation_design.adoc`): events of
the key under activation that arrive during the activation are handed to the running loop as
observations. Per-key ordering makes this safe, since only the running activation can see them.
Which event types interrupt which activations is declared per subscription in the activator,
never inferred by the model; with the activator's one-key-at-a-time rule, deferral is the
default for every type not declared. The Planner declares its own request type,
`planning.ItineraryRequested`, as interrupting. The delivery loop never stops fetching, so a
newer request for the trip under activation waits in the lane's queue; the running activation
takes it from there between reasoning steps, abandons the run, answers the older request with
`planning.ItineraryRequestSuperseded` (causation: the newer request), and starts the newer one
from its checkpoint. Both records' offsets commit when the activation returns, so a crash
redelivers both in order and the rerun supersedes again. A request arriving after the
activation has finished is an ordinary event and the trip gets a second proposal.

*Demonstration.* The Planner is mid-assembly of a multi-city trip; the customer writes "make it
the 14th to the 17th"; the Front Desk writes `CustomerReplied`, the Booking Agent commits
`TripChanged` and asks planning again; the Planner's running activation takes the new request
from its inbox between tool steps, publishes `ItineraryRequestSuperseded` for the old one,
restarts from its checkpoint with the new requirements, and carries over the items the request
names as unchanged. The book's other cases stay
activation-level and are named beside it: hydration at wake-up (6.3), the semantic checkpoint
resumed with the new fact (8.2), the stale input rejected (8.5), the loop stopped from outside
(9.1).

=== W2: Inbound Email Triage

Email arrives in volume; most of it must never reach an expensive agent.

*Flow:* `customer.EmailReceived` (the replay script over the seeded dataset). The Semantic
Router buffers each burst in a short time window, sorts the window by sender timestamp (the
ingress re-sequencer, one line, and the deterministic fix that works only when a trusted
sequence exists), and classifies the batch in one bulk call (semantic windowing). What it
recognises it writes directly as the serving context's request, in that context's published
language: `booking.TripRequested` for a trip request, `support.SupportRequested` for a
question, `destination.InspirationRequested` for a customer who has not decided where to go;
a supplier's email (a hotel's confirmation) is the edge observation
`booking.SupplierUpdateReceived`. It strips the raw email (headers, quoted chains) down to the
fields the request needs (payload mediation); spam is dropped at the Token Defense Filter. An
out-of-order supplier update (a hotel confirmation arriving before its prerequisite flight
confirmation) is staged in the `InboxStore` until the prerequisite arrives: the Agentic Inbox,
framed as an agent's activation gated on the completeness of its context, verified
deterministically before any inference runs. NACK and retry is analyzed in prose and not built:
bouncing an event through a consumer that runs a model re-runs the model, and a nack fights
per-key ordering everywhere (block the key, as queue brokers do, or break the order, as a retry
topic does). The port stays as it is: return acknowledges, raise redelivers under
at-least-once.

The Router records the trip-to-thread link, and the email delivery adapter follows the trip's
facts by trip id and replies on the thread, recording `customer.CustomerNotified`: delivery is
one pattern with one adapter per channel. The Router runs as two replicas from ch 4: competing
consumption, and with `partitionkey` (the conversation id) per-key serialization. The Router
and the Front Desk's classifier do the same job in two components, and ch 4 says why: an
interactive single message against a windowed burst with competing consumers.

*Built in:* ch 4 (support integration itself from ch 3).

=== W3: Destination Knowledge, Ingestion and Inspiration

*Flow:* `destination.BrochureUploaded` (UI or script); the PDF is stored via the `BlobStore`;
`destination.DocumentAvailable` carries only the claim reference; the Destination Research
Agent (LlamaIndex) indexes it and answers the Planner's questions over A2A from indexed
knowledge, a synchronous cross-context query permitted because the answer is perishable and
nobody else acts on it. Separately, `destination.InspirationRequested` (the Front Desk's
classifier or the Router, on a vague request) wakes the Destination Inspiration crew (CrewAI),
whose personas turn the indexed brochures into a themed suggestion pack,
`destination.SuggestionPackReady`, which the Front Desk shows the customer; a customer who then
decides starts W1.

*Built in:* ch 3 (the Research Agent over A2A), ch 4 (the crew, routed to by meaning), ch 5
(claim check).

=== W4: B2B Policy and Approval

*Flow:* `policy.PolicyViolationDetected` (the Policy Gate, on `ItineraryApproved`, against the
corporate client's seeded rules; e.g., a flight over $1000). The Booking Agent saves
`AWAITING_POLICY_APPROVAL` and emits `booking.ApprovalRequested` **atomically via the Agentic
Outbox**, carrying the semantic checkpoint (the reasoning snapshot, including the Planner's
rationale carried in the proposal). The approval console, a policy-context app, opens the
ApprovalRequest, the manager's record of the out-of-policy trip (the violations, the assigned
approver, the deadline), and the manager decides: `policy.ApprovalGranted` or
`policy.ApprovalRejected`. Or the `EventScheduler` fires `booking.ApprovalTimedOut` after 48 h
on the Booking Agent's behalf, and the workflow escalates. A stale approval arriving after the
timeout is the ABA demonstration.

The dual key (13.5) is stated here and enforced by the reservation step: a high-risk side
effect requires two independent keys, the Policy Gate's `PolicyCheckPassed` and the manager's
decision on the ApprovalRequest, and the agent's own reasoning is neither. Ch 13 hardens the
same gate against injected prompts and blinds the Planner's sub-agents and the Negotiator to
traveler documents (contextual segregation).

*Built in:* ch 8 (ch 13 hardening).

=== W5: Disruption and Compensation Saga

The unhappy path as a business reality: the trip is confirmed and the world changes.

*Flow:* the manager approves, the trip confirms (ch 8's sense), then the Hotel Reservation
System sim's scripted `servicing.HotelSoldOut` arrives through the sim's callback adapter, a
business event from the environment. Entitlements gate the re-plan and the customer's message
needs the re-plan, so only part of the work runs in parallel. The Policy Gate determines what
the customer is owed, first and deterministically, as `policy.EntitlementDetermined`. Then two
things run at once: the Booking Agent asks planning for a replacement within that envelope
(`planning.ItineraryRequested`, naming the stay to replace), and the Recovery
Agent (ADK) assesses the trip through the Observer's view and drafts
`servicing.CompensationDrafted`, the customer's message and a compensation plan computed from
the entitlement, which does not depend on which replacement is chosen. After the barrier the
Booking Agent assembles both into `booking.RebookingProposed`, and the customer accepts
(approval, reservation, confirmation) or declines. On a decline, or when no replacement exists,
the Booking Agent's deterministic compensation step executes the mutations, cancelling the
flight on the Airline Reservation System sim (`booking.FlightReservationCancelled`) and, from
ch 10, refunding through the payment provider adapter (`booking.RefundRequested`,
`booking.PaymentRefunded`), and closes the workflow with `booking.TripCancelled`. The draft is
an immutable payload superseded by a new draft if the plan is rejected.

If a replacement stay must be argued with a hotel whose agent never converges (the sim's
non-converging auto-responder), the platform's livelock guard counts turns per `correlationid`
in the `GuardStateStore`, the shape of ch 11's token budget guard, and publishes
`system.LivelockDetected`, consumed by the Recovery Agent into the same compensation path. The
counter is a guard over the thread, not state of any aggregate.

*Built in:* ch 9 (on ch 8's machinery; the refund step with ch 10's payment step).

==== The ch 9 deadlock scenario

A cycle between two contexts, each waiting on a locally sensible rule. A misconfigured policy
rule prices the entitlement from the replacement's cost, so the Policy Gate waits for the
re-plan's proposal before publishing `EntitlementDetermined`; the Booking Agent waits for
`EntitlementDetermined` before it issues the re-plan request, as the workflow above requires.
Both stopped, and the Planner, with its one inbound type, is never a party. The `EventScheduler`
macro-timeout on the Booking Agent's wait breaks the cycle: the Booking Agent proceeds with the
default entitlement and the chapter shows the rule that caused it. The scenario runs on ch 8's
scheduler and lives in ch 9's practical. Whether the hotel's agent stalls or loops is a
different failure with a different guard (the livelock guard above).

=== W6: Failure Recovery and Replay

*Flow:* a prompt change ships to the Planner (a compose file selects a new prompt version) and
its proposals start failing outbound contract validation at the activator boundary. Each failed
event parks on the dead-letter queue with its key preserved, so later events for the same trip
do not overtake it. A prompt is a versioned artifact and the fix is a version bump: the replay
tool selects parked events by type and by the `promptversion` that produced them, republishes
them, and the Planner on the fixed prompt emits valid proposals. The audit record shows both
attempts on one correlation id. Separately, a duplicate delivery of `booking.PaymentRequested`
is absorbed by the payment provider adapter's `IdempotencyStore` check before the Payment sim
is charged twice.

*Built in:* ch 10.

=== S1: Trip Memory and Context (supporting workflow)

*Flow:* all W1 events reach the Observer/Reflector Agent, which projects `TripView` upserts into
the `SemanticViewStore`, keyed by the trip id; any waking agent hydrates by the trip id through
the context path (`/trips/{trip_id}/...`, Tier 1 + Tier 2) instead of replaying history, with
`correlationid` scoping the current thread only, since a second thread on the same trip generates
its own. Ch 5's A/B compares the summarizing and experiential variants of the same Observer,
both running with no store behind them; ch 6 selects the store and the Planner's hydration
adapter; ch 7 scopes views by `contextpath` (`/trips/{trip_id}/...`) so the Paris hotel work
never sees Madrid's events, and files the experiential variant's lessons under the path where
they were learned.

*The scenario ch 6 solves.* Before ch 6 the Planner runs on two things: the request it
consumes, which carries the requirements as they now stand and the customer's last reason, and
its own store of earlier proposals. That is enough for a fresh request and for
one rejection. It is not enough for a thread that drifts. A customer rejects round one as too
expensive; changes the dates in round two; says in round three that the hotel from round one
was fine and only the flight was the problem; and meanwhile tells support, in a separate
conversation on another topic, that they cannot fly before ten in the morning. Each
`ItineraryRequested` carries the current requirements and the latest reason, and nothing
carries the thread: the Planner re-plans against the last reason only, reintroduces what round
one rejected, never sees the support fact, and the proposals oscillate between two shapes until
the customer gives up after seven rounds. From ch 6 the Observer, which sees every topic,
projects the conversation's summary into the `TripView`: what was rejected and why, every
constraint stated across the rounds, the support fact. The Planner hydrates it at wake-up, and
round three's proposal respects everything said so far; the thread converges in four rounds.
The demonstration runs the same recorded conversation twice, without and with the store, and
the two audit records differ.

*Built in:* ch 5 (variants), ch 6 (CQRS + hydration), ch 7 (context paths, lessons learned).

=== S2: Duty of Care (supporting workflow)

*Flow:* `system.DutyOfCareSweepDue` (EventScheduler, recurring) wakes the Duty-of-Care Monitor,
which queries the Travel Advisory Feed sim for advisories touching reserved destinations and
publishes `servicing.TravelRiskDetected` on impact; the Booking Agent records the warning as a
trip fact the Front Desk shows, whose `customer.CustomerNotified` is the record that the
traveler was told; a disruptive advisory can open the W5 compensation path. The Monitor is
stateless: it publishes a detection and never a trip fact. The Booking Agent's follow-up nudge
shares the mechanism: `booking.CustomerFollowUpDue`, scheduled when `ProposalReady` is
published, triggers a reminder if no `CustomerReplied` has arrived.

*Built in:* ch 8.

=== Cross-cutting layers (ch 10 to 15)

Not workflows but behaviors applied across W1 to W6:

* **Reliability (ch 10):** DLQ routing and replay by prompt version (W6), idempotent side
  effects; the payment step and the refund step join W1 and W5; `promptversion` and
  `modelversion` as extension attributes.
* **Cost governance (ch 11):** the token budget per `correlationid` in the `GuardStateStore`
  and `BudgetExceeded` throttling are the book's own pattern; predictive throttling against
  provider limits and the fallback chain (cloud, then local Ollama) are LiteLLM configuration,
  and a LiteLLM callback publishes `ModelFallbackActivated` so the fallback is a visible fact,
  not a silent retry; the supplier circuit breaker on the shared supplier client, demonstrated
  on the Airline Reservation System sim's slow-vendor mode, returns its open state to the agent
  as a "supplier unavailable" observation for the reasoning loop to adapt to, so an agent's two
  failing dependencies have two breakers.
* **Testing (ch 12):** the whole system exercised offline: `InMemoryBroker`, deterministic LLM
  fixtures (which also pin the Paris lesson's wording), LLM-as-judge regression scoring, a
  shadow consumer (a V2 Semantic Router classifier beside the live one, its classifications
  routed to the evaluation sink), and ch 7's audit recording as the historical event file. Two
  test kinds on the Planner: feasibility is a contract test at the validating boundary,
  deterministic; optimality is a semantic regression, the same recorded `ItineraryRequested`
  replayed through a new prompt version and the proposal compared with the recorded one by the
  judge.
* **Security (ch 13):** the `identitytoken` attribute verified at the activator boundary and by
  the Guardrail Worker on sensitive topics; the Guardrail Worker's republish-or-quarantine
  (`EventQuarantined`); egress DLP scrubbing on the support vendor and the hotels' agents; the
  Policy Gate as the deterministic last line and the dual key stated; traveler documents as the
  segregated niche and the Negotiator's bounded brief as the reason; the disclosure question
  (an agent asked "are you an AI?" by a hotel's clerk); the red-team script publishes a forged
  `booking.TripConfirmed` and watches it rejected.
* **Observability and evolution (ch 14):** OTel spans (`llm.token_count`,
  `agent.reasoning_step`) across W1 end to end; the partner adapter writing
  `booking.TripRequested` V2 (traveller documents, a loyalty programme) and the Translator
  serving V1 to the Booking Agent, immutable and unchanged, through a deprecation window. The
  second case is a new member of a union: a rail item joins the flight and the stay in
  `planning.ItineraryProposed`, and a consumer validating with the old classes rejects the
  whole proposal on the unknown kind.
* **Cloud (ch 15):** every Port re-implemented against managed services that order per key; the
  same workflows under load and chaos.

== Draft Event Catalog

Conventions:

* Names are **PascalCase past-tense business facts** (`FlightReserved`), never commands. A
  request is a fact too: `TripRequested` records that a trip is wanted, which cannot be
  rejected, only answered or left unanswered.
* On the wire, every event is a CloudEvent; `type` is `<domain>.<Name>` (e.g.
  `booking.TripRequested`), where `<domain>` is the bounded context, the catalog's group headers
  below (`customer`, `planning`, `booking`, `policy`, `servicing`, `support`, `destination`);
  the domain map in 01_system_overview.adoc is the authority, and `system` is the technical
  namespace outside it. The book's prose uses the same qualified form. Payload schemas are
  Pydantic models (`ch02_semantic_data_contracts`), one `@event` class per event in
  `travel_agency.events`, each bound to its type and its key
  (`04_implementation_design.adoc`, The Agent Activator); this catalog fixes names and
  ownership, not field lists, since full contract detail would be invalidated by
  chapter-level discoveries.
* Every event carries `correlationid` and `partitionkey` (both from ch 2), `contextpath` (from
  ch 7), `promptversion` and `modelversion` on what an agent publishes (from ch 10), and
  `identitytoken` (from ch 13) as CloudEvents extension attributes. `correlationid` is the
  conversation's id. `partitionkey` is the id of the event's aggregate: the trip's id on
  booking, planning, and policy facts, the conversation's id on customer facts, and, where the
  context holds no aggregate, the trip concerned or, before a trip exists, the conversation.
  Ordering by key is part of the broker contract: events sharing a `partitionkey` are
  delivered in publish order, one at a time, to one consumer, and all of a context's events
  travel on one topic (the adapter's mapping from ch 1). There are no sequence numbers and no
  per-event ordinal: order is the transport's, per key, never the consumer's by content. The
  full identity conventions (UUID event ids, causation, the trip's id, the customer reference)
  are the Identifiers section of 01_system_overview.adoc.
* One owning context per event type: the producer named stands in that context, or relays for
  it (the `EventScheduler`, the outbox relay) with `source` naming the owner. The exception is
  a context's requests, its open-host published language, written by whoever asks under the
  serving context's prefix (marked *open host* below). An edge component's observation
  (`RateNegotiated`, `CarRentalReserved`, `PaymentAuthorized`, `SupplierUpdateReceived`) is an
  input to the aggregate's writer, recorded by a fact of the writer's own. Consumers listed are
  typical, not exclusive: the audit consumer and the Observer see everything via the port's
  subscribe-all capability (01_system_overview.adoc, Ports).

Notes:

* Chapter 1 publishes two event types, `planning.ItineraryRequested` (demo script to Planner
  and audit) and `planning.ItineraryProposed` (Planner to the script), the reply carrying the
  request event's id as a `request_id` payload field, promoted to a `causationid` extension
  attribute in `ch02_correlation_id`, and `proposal_id`, generated by the Planner, joining the
  reply with the ch 2 contract. From there both keep their shape for the whole book: the ch 1
  Planner publishes one itinerary with items and no offers, and the ch 2 Planner fills the same
  shape.
* The customer's decision (`CustomerReplied`, `ItineraryApproved`, `ItineraryRejected`,
  `TripChanged`) debuts in ch 3 with the Booking Agent: acceptance is a fact whether or not
  anything reserves on it yet, and ch 6's scenario needs rejections from ch 3. `TripConfirmed`
  debuts in ch 8 with the customer reference, and until ch 10 confirmation means the
  reservations confirmed with payment offstage.
* DLQ and replay are broker-contract behavior (ch 10), not separate event types: dead-lettered
  events are the original business events on a DLQ topic, selected for replay by type and
  `promptversion`.
