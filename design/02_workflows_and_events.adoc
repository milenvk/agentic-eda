== Workflow Catalog

Six business workflows (W1 to W6), two supporting workflows (S1, S2), and the cross-cutting
layers of chapters 10 to 15. Each workflow is an event-flow narrative; "built in" lists the
chapters that construct it. A workflow built across several chapters is the norm, and is the
evolutionary architecture working as promised. Event names are defined in the catalog at the end
of this document; the nouns are the glossary's in 01_system_overview.adoc.

=== W1: Trip Booking (the spine)

The one workflow that runs through the whole book. Every part extends it rather than replacing
it. Intake is its first step, not a workflow of its own: until the request is published the
conversation belongs to Customer Relations, and the Booking Agent's partial understanding of it
is private state.

*End-state flow:*

1. **Intake.** The Booking Agent recognizes a trip request in a channel message (the Front Desk
   shell's `customer.ChatMessageReceived`, or the Semantic Router's
   `customer.TripRequestRecognized` when the request arrives by email; in ch 1 the demo script
   publishes the request directly) and publishes `booking.TripRequested`. That event's id is
   the trip's id and its `partitionkey`; the Booking Agent records the trip under it, and the
   Planner reacts to the same event in parallel.
2. **Planning, simple trip.** The Itinerary Planner consumes the request, consults the
   Destination Research Agent, queries availability on the Airline Reservation System sim, and
   publishes `planning.ItineraryProposed`, version 1 of the itinerary keyed by the trip.
3. **Planning, multi-item trip.** The Planner instead fans out `FlightSearchRequested` and
   `HotelSearchRequested`; the Airline and Hotel Agents answer with `FlightOffersProposed` and
   `HotelOffersProposed`, each offer carrying its validity and a hotel offer its rooms left;
   the Planner's barrier releases when both are in; conflicting details go through
   `ActionProposed` and `ActionCommitted` (Arbiter); the Planner then publishes
   `ItineraryProposed` for the assembled itinerary, with a backup item when a hotel offer is
   flagged as the last room. The Planner is the only consumer of `TripRequested` and decides
   which path a request takes.
4. **Relay and decision.** The Booking Agent relays the proposal, with its offers' validity,
   through the shell (`customer.CustomerNotified` records the delivery) and, from ch 8,
   schedules `CustomerFollowUpDue`. The customer accepts (`booking.ItineraryApproved`, naming
   the proposal by its event id), rejects (`booking.ItineraryRejected`, and the Planner
   re-plans as a new version), or changes the request (`booking.TripChanged`, and the Planner
   re-plans the affected items only; arriving mid-activation it interrupts, see below).
5. **Policy.** On `ItineraryApproved` the Policy Gate reads the corporate client's seeded rules
   and either stays silent or publishes `policy.PolicyViolationDetected`, which opens W4. The
   reservation step runs only on the gate's pass and, after a violation, the manager's
   decision: two independent keys, neither of them the agent's own reasoning.
6. **Reservation step.** The Booking Agent's deterministic reservation step reserves what the
   accepted itinerary contains, on the Airline and Hotel Reservation System sims, in whatever
   order or parallelism its instructions fix: `FlightReserved` and `HotelReserved`, each a
   hold with an expiry. A reservation no longer granted by the supplier is `ReservationFailed`;
   the step falls back to the itinerary's backup item when it carries one, and otherwise the
   Booking Agent asks the customer. The Rental Car Agent reacts to `FlightReserved` by pure
   choreography: a pay-later `CarRentalReserved` in the requested class with its estimated
   total, or `CarRentalUnavailable` when no class fits; the Booking Agent records the car on
   the trip with `CarReserved`, causation set to the car event. The trip is now reserved.
7. **Payment** (from ch 10). The Booking Agent publishes `booking.PaymentRequested`; the
   payment provider adapter charges the Payment sim under an idempotency key and answers with
   `booking.PaymentAuthorized`; the Booking Agent records the Payment entity on the trip.
8. **Confirmation.** `booking.TripConfirmed` (Booking Agent), carrying the customer reference:
   the holds are converted to confirmed reservations with the suppliers. The Front Desk shell
   delivers it to the customer and publishes `customer.CustomerNotified`. In ch 8 and ch 9
   confirmation means the reservations confirmed, with payment offstage; ch 10 inserts step 7
   before it, and the refund step of W5 with it.

*Built in:* ch 1 (steps 1 and 2 decoupled as a round trip: the demo script publishes
`TripRequested` and receives `ItineraryProposed`, matching on the request event's id; audit
consumer), ch 2 (CloudEvents contracts, correlation and causation, `partitionkey`, one topic per
context), ch 3 (planning against the Airline Reservation System sim; the Booking Agent and the
shell take over step 1 for chat), ch 4 (step 1 for email), ch 7 (steps 3, 4, and 6: the
demonstration ends with the trip reserved, both holds in place), ch 8 (steps 5 and 8, the
follow-up nudge), ch 10 (step 7, idempotent).

==== The ch 7 demonstration: two Paris runs

Ch 7's practical runs W1 twice through steps 3, 4, and 6, with every agent up, no seeding, and
real events; the audit consumer's recording of the first run is ch 12's historical event file
and an optional replay shortcut for readers who skip the first run. The demonstration uses
only what the book has introduced by ch 7, and it does not spend ch 9's lesson early: a lapsing
hold is not a saga.

*First run.* A customer asks for two travelers, New York to Paris, 12 to 15 March, a hotel in
the 8th arrondissement. The Planner fans out; the Hotel Agent returns one room in the 8th,
flagged as the last room from the sim's rooms-left field; the Arbiter settles arrival against
check-in; the itinerary is relayed with its offers' validity. The customer approves after
twenty-five minutes. The reservation step holds the flight, and the hotel reservation fails
(`ReservationFailed`): the last room is sold. The Booking Agent relays "sold out, your flight
is held until 18:00, shall I look nearby?"; the customer answers "try the 16th", published by the
Booking Agent as `TripChanged`. The Planner re-plans the hotel item only, a new
itinerary version; the flight hold carries over; the customer approves the new version; the
hotel is reserved; the run ends with the trip reserved, both holds in place. The human fixed
it.

*The lesson.* A practice, not a frequency claim, as a human agent would draw it from one case.
The experiential Observer writes under `/trips/*/paris/hotel`: a hotel offer flagged as the last
room was gone by the time the customer approved; when a hotel offer is flagged last room, carry
a backup hotel in the proposal and tell the customer the offer's validity. The lesson is filed
where it was learned, and the path scoping is the epistemic humility: one case earns a local
practice, not a global rule. The lesson's wording varies by run; ch 12's fixtures pin it in
tests.

*Second run.* Another customer asks for the 8th on similar dates. The Hotel Agent wakes with the
lesson and proposes the 8th room, flagged last room, with a backup in the 16th; the Booking
Agent relays the validity; the customer is slow again; the reservation step tries the 8th,
fails the same way, reserves the backup without asking, and the Booking Agent tells the
customer; the run ends with the trip reserved. The world did not change; the handling did.

*What the design supplies:* a rooms-left field on hotel offers; a backup item in the itinerary;
the reservation step falling back to a backup on failure; `booking.ReservationFailed`; hold
expiry as a reservation status; a partial re-plan as a new itinerary version.

==== The ch 7 interrupt: a trip change mid-planning

A relevant fact can arrive while a long activation runs, and the agent should revise its
beliefs rather than finish on stale ones. The book's vocabulary is *interrupting events*, with
three strategies named: defer to the next activation (safe, and stale), inject the fact as an
observation at the next reasoning step (belief revision inside the loop), or abort and restart
from the checkpoint plus the fact (the boundary event, wasteful of tokens). Other traditions
have their own names: belief revision with intention reconsideration (BDI), the interrupting
boundary event (BPMN), a signal to a running workflow (Temporal), an interrupt (agent
frameworks, usually meaning a pause for human input).

The mechanism is the container's activation inbox (`04_implementation_design.adoc`): events of
the key under activation that arrive during the activation are handed to the running loop as
observations. Per-key ordering makes this safe, since only the running activation can see them.
Which event types interrupt which activations is declared per subscription in the container,
never inferred by the model; with the container's one-key-at-a-time rule, deferral is the
default for every type not declared.

*Demonstration.* The Planner is mid-assembly of a multi-city trip; the customer writes "make it
the 14th to the 17th"; the Booking Agent publishes `booking.TripChanged`; the Planner's running
activation receives it between tool steps and re-plans. The book's other cases stay
activation-level and are named beside it: hydration at wake-up (6.3), the semantic checkpoint
resumed with the new fact (8.2), the stale input rejected (8.5), the loop stopped from outside
(9.1).

=== W2: Inbound Email Triage

Email arrives in volume; most of it must never reach an expensive agent.

*Flow:* `customer.EmailReceived` (the replay script over the seeded dataset). The Semantic
Router buffers each burst in a short time window, sorts the window by sender timestamp (the
ingress re-sequencer, one line, and the deterministic fix that works only when a trusted
sequence exists), and classifies the batch in one bulk call (semantic windowing). It
republishes a typed recognition event of its own context, `customer.TripRequestRecognized`,
`customer.SupportRequestRecognized`, or `customer.VendorUpdateRecognized`, each carrying the
conversation id, and strips the raw email (headers, quoted chains) down to the fields needed
downstream (payload mediation); spam is dropped at the Token Defense Filter. An out-of-order
vendor update (a hotel confirmation arriving before its prerequisite flight confirmation) is
staged in the `InboxStore` until the prerequisite arrives: the Agentic Inbox, framed as an
agent's activation gated on the completeness of its context, verified deterministically before
any inference runs. NACK and retry is analyzed in prose and not built: bouncing an event through
a consumer that runs a model re-runs the model, and a nack fights per-key ordering everywhere
(block the key, as queue brokers do, or break the order, as a retry topic does). The port
stays as it is: return acknowledges, raise redelivers under at-least-once.

The Booking Agent turns `TripRequestRecognized` into `booking.TripRequested`, since that type
has one owning context. Support requests leave the Router as `SupportRequestRecognized`; the
support adapter republishes `support.SupportRequested` as its own type and hands the request
to the external Support Desk AaaS sim via Deterministic Edge Prompting (`SupportTicketResolved`
comes back). The Router runs as two replicas from ch 4: competing consumption, and with
`partitionkey` (the conversation id) per-key serialization.

*Built in:* ch 4 (support integration itself from ch 3).

=== W3: Destination Research and Content Ingestion

*Flow:* `destination.BrochureUploaded` (UI or script); the PDF is stored via the `BlobStore`;
`destination.DocumentAvailable` carries only the claim reference; the Destination Research
Agent (LlamaIndex) indexes it; planning queries, over MCP, answer from indexed knowledge.

*Built in:* ch 5 (claim check); the agent itself from ch 3.

=== W4: B2B Policy and Approval

*Flow:* `policy.PolicyViolationDetected` (the Policy Gate, on `ItineraryApproved`, against the
corporate client's seeded rules; e.g., a flight over $1000). The Booking Agent saves
`WAITING_FOR_APPROVAL` and emits `booking.ApprovalRequested` **atomically via the Agentic
Outbox**, carrying the semantic checkpoint (the reasoning snapshot, including the Planner's
rationale carried in the itinerary event). The approval console opens the ApprovalRequest, the
manager's record of the out-of-policy trip (the violations, the assigned approver, the
deadline), and the manager decides: `policy.ApprovalGranted` or `policy.ApprovalRejected`. Or
the `EventScheduler` fires `booking.ApprovalTimedOut` after 48 h on the Booking Agent's behalf,
and the workflow escalates. A stale approval arriving after the timeout is the ABA
demonstration.

The dual key (13.5) is stated here and enforced by the reservation step: a high-risk side
effect requires two independent keys, the Policy Gate's deterministic pass and the manager's
decision on the ApprovalRequest, and the agent's own reasoning is neither. Ch 13 hardens the
same gate against injected prompts and blinds the search agents to traveler documents
(contextual segregation).

*Built in:* ch 8 (ch 13 hardening).

=== W5: Disruption and Compensation Saga

The unhappy path as a business reality: the trip is confirmed and the world changes.

*Flow:* the manager approves, the trip confirms (ch 8's sense), then the Hotel Reservation
System sim's scripted `servicing.HotelSoldOut` arrives through the sim's callback adapter. The
Cleanup Agent (LangGraph) catches the failure event, assesses the trip through the Observer's
view, and drafts `servicing.CancellationDrafted`: a nuanced customer email and a compensation
plan, an immutable payload superseded by a new draft if the plan is rejected. The Booking
Agent's deterministic compensation step executes the mutations, cancelling the flight on the
Airline Reservation System sim (`booking.FlightReservationCancelled`) and, from ch 10,
refunding through the payment provider adapter (`booking.RefundRequested`,
`booking.PaymentRefunded`), and closes the workflow with `booking.TripCancelled`.

Rebooking first, where the disruption allows it: the Hotel Agent negotiates with the hotel
sim's scripted auto-responder. If the negotiation loops, the platform's livelock guard counts
turns per `correlationid` in the `WorkflowStateStore`, the shape of ch 11's token budget guard,
and publishes `system.LivelockDetected`, consumed by the Cleanup Agent into the same
compensation path. The counter is a guard over the thread, not state of any aggregate.

*Built in:* ch 9 (on ch 8's machinery; the refund step with ch 10's payment step).

==== The ch 9 deadlock scenario

After a sold-out on a multi-city trip, the rebooking sub-workflow stalls in a circular wait: the
Hotel Agent waits for the new flight's arrival time before choosing check-in, while the Airline
Agent waits for the hotel's confirmed dates before choosing the flight, both stopped at
`ActionProposed`. The localized sub-orchestrator (the Planner over the rebooking sub-workflow)
resolves it with a macro-timeout through the `EventScheduler` that forces the Arbiter to commit
defaults (`ActionCommitted`), after which both agents proceed. The scenario runs on ch 7's
propose-commit and ch 8's scheduler and lives in ch 9's practical.

=== W6: Failure Recovery and Replay

*Flow:* a malformed reservation confirmation (poison pill) crashes the CrewAI Hotel Agent; the
event lands on the DLQ; the bug is fixed; the same pure business event is replayed to a
LangGraph-built replacement agent, which finishes the trip. Separately, a duplicate delivery of
`booking.PaymentRequested` is absorbed by the payment provider adapter's `IdempotencyStore`
check before the Payment sim is charged twice.

*Built in:* ch 10.

=== S1: Trip Memory and Context (supporting workflow)

*Flow:* all W1 events reach the Observer/Reflector Agent, which projects `TripView` upserts into
the `SemanticViewStore`; any waking agent hydrates by `correlationid` and `contextpath` (Tier 1
+ Tier 2) instead of replaying history. Ch 5's A/B compares the summarizing and experiential
variants of the same Observer; ch 7 scopes views by `contextpath` (`/trips/{trip_id}/...`) so
the Paris hotel work never sees Madrid's events, and files the experiential variant's lessons
under the path where they were learned.

*Built in:* ch 5 (variants), ch 6 (CQRS + hydration), ch 7 (context paths, lessons learned).

=== S2: Duty of Care (supporting workflow)

*Flow:* `system.DutyOfCareSweepDue` (EventScheduler, recurring) wakes the Duty-of-Care Monitor,
which queries the Travel Advisory Feed sim for advisories touching reserved destinations and
publishes `servicing.TravelRiskDetected` on impact; the Booking Agent warns the customer through
the Front Desk shell, whose `customer.CustomerNotified` is the record that the traveler was
told; a disruptive advisory can open the W5 compensation path. The Monitor is stateless: it
publishes a detection and never a trip fact. The Booking Agent's follow-up nudge shares the
mechanism: `booking.CustomerFollowUpDue`, scheduled when a proposal is relayed, triggers a
reminder if the customer has not answered.

*Built in:* ch 8.

=== Cross-cutting layers (ch 10 to 15)

Not workflows but behaviors applied across W1 to W6:

* **Reliability (ch 10):** DLQ routing and replay (W6), idempotent side effects; the payment
  step and the refund step join W1 and W5.
* **Cost governance (ch 11):** the token budget per `correlationid` in the `WorkflowStateStore`
  and `BudgetExceeded` throttling are the book's own pattern; predictive throttling against
  provider limits and the fallback chain (cloud, then local Ollama) are LiteLLM configuration,
  and a LiteLLM callback publishes `ModelFallbackActivated` so the fallback is a visible fact,
  not a silent retry.
* **Testing (ch 12):** the whole system exercised offline: `InMemoryBroker`, deterministic LLM
  fixtures (which also pin the Paris lesson's wording), LLM-as-judge regression scoring, a
  shadow consumer, and ch 7's audit recording as the historical event file.
* **Security (ch 13):** the `identitytoken` attribute verified at the container boundary and by
  the Guardrail Worker on sensitive topics; the Guardrail Worker's republish-or-quarantine
  (`EventQuarantined`); egress DLP scrubbing; the Policy Gate as the deterministic last line and
  the dual key stated; traveler documents as the segregated niche; the red-team script
  publishes a forged `booking.TripConfirmed` and watches it rejected.
* **Observability and evolution (ch 14):** OTel spans (`llm.token_count`,
  `agent.reasoning_step`) across W1 end to end; the Translator serving a V1 consumer of
  `planning.ItineraryProposed` (ch 1's flat shape) through a deprecation window while V2
  producers (ch 7's itemized shape) run.
* **Cloud (ch 15):** every Port re-implemented against managed services that order by key; the
  same workflows under load and chaos.

== Draft Event Catalog

Conventions:

* Names are **PascalCase past-tense business facts** (`FlightReserved`), never commands.
* On the wire, every event is a CloudEvent; `type` is `<domain>.<Name>` (e.g.
  `booking.TripRequested`), where `<domain>` is the bounded context, the catalog's group headers
  below (`customer`, `planning`, `booking`, `policy`, `servicing`, `support`, `destination`);
  the domain map in 01_system_overview.adoc is the authority, and `system` is the technical
  namespace outside it. The book's prose uses the same qualified form. Payload schemas are
  Pydantic models (`ch02_semantic_data_contracts`); this catalog fixes names and ownership,
  not field lists, since full contract detail would be invalidated by chapter-level
  discoveries.
* Every event carries `correlationid` and `partitionkey` (both from ch 2) and `contextpath`
  (from ch 7) as CloudEvents extension attributes, and `identitytoken` from ch 13.
  `partitionkey` is the id of the event's aggregate or, where the context holds no aggregate,
  the id of the trip concerned; on `booking.TripRequested` it is the event's own id. Ordering
  by key is part of the broker contract: events sharing a `partitionkey` are delivered in
  publish order, one at a time, to one consumer, and all of a context's events travel on one
  topic (the adapter's mapping from the ch 2 practical). There are no sequence numbers and no
  per-event ordinal: order is the transport's, per key, never the consumer's by content. The
  full identity conventions (UUID event ids, causation, the trip's id, the customer reference)
  are the Identifiers section of 01_system_overview.adoc.
* One owning context per event type (single writer): the producer named stands in that
  context, or relays for it (the `EventScheduler`, the outbox relay) with `source` naming the
  owner. A request carries the requester's prefix and its answer the answerer's. An edge
  component's observation (`CarRentalReserved`, `PaymentAuthorized`) is an input to the
  aggregate's writer, recorded by a fact of the writer's own. Consumers listed are typical,
  not exclusive: the audit consumer and the Observer see everything via the port's
  subscribe-all capability (01_system_overview.adoc, Ports).

Notes:

* Chapter 1 publishes two event types, `TripRequested` (demo script to Planner and audit) and
  `ItineraryProposed` (Planner to the script), the reply carrying the request event's id as a
  `request_id` payload field, promoted to a `causationid` extension attribute in
  `ch02_correlation_id`.
* `ItineraryApproved` debuts in ch 7 because the customer's acceptance precedes the reservation
  step and the Paris demonstration needs it; `TripConfirmed` debuts in ch 8 with the customer
  reference, and until ch 10 confirmation means the reservations confirmed with payment
  offstage.
* DLQ and replay are broker-contract behavior (ch 10), not separate event types: dead-lettered
  events are the original business events on a DLQ topic.
